# OURORIGIN

## Founder’s Creative Direction

### Identity, Mark & Wordmark Development Brief

**Working principle: Do not decorate the idea. Discover its visual form.**

---

# 01. WHY WE ARE REDOING THIS

The current OurOrigin mark is **not the final logo**.

It is a conceptual exploration of an important idea: the human act of recording, counting, witnessing and completing something.

The current tally direction is strategically relevant, but the task now is different.

We are not asking:

> “How can we make this tally look better?”

We are asking:

> **“What should OurOrigin actually look like if it becomes a globally recognisable identity?”**

The current concept may survive that investigation.

It may not.

We must be willing to discover that.

The final identity must be strong enough that the explanation becomes secondary.

---

# 02. THE BRAND WE ARE DESIGNING FOR

OurOrigin is not simply an ethical-food company.

The fundamental proposition is:

> **Reduce the distance between origin and consumption.**

The business exists to make origin visible and meaningful.

The person who grows, gathers or produces something is not an anonymous “beneficiary.”

They are the author of the product.

The system is therefore built around:

* First Hands
* direct transaction
* transparent pricing
* place
* nature
* evidence
* human knowledge
* participation

The existing brand specification describes OurOrigin as a conduit rather than a conventional brand and establishes the “First Hands” principle as foundational.

The proof system further grounds the brand in grower identity, price paid, nature's share and geographical coordinates.

This is the territory.

The logo does **not** need to depict all of it.

---

# 03. THE CENTRAL DESIGN QUESTION

## What visual gesture can become synonymous with OurOrigin?

Not:

* agriculture
* farming
* Kerala
* spices
* leaves
* hands
* soil
* mountains
* villages
* maps
* sustainability
* “community”
* generic connection

Those are categories.

We need an **identity**.

A strong identity should allow someone to eventually see the mark without the word “OurOrigin” and think:

> OurOrigin.

That recognition will come from repeated exposure, but the mark must first possess enough **distinctiveness and character** to be worth remembering.

---

# 04. THE MOST IMPORTANT PRINCIPLE

## Meaning → Behaviour → Form

Do not begin with an icon.

Begin with the brand's underlying behaviour.

### Meaning

Origin is visible.

### Behaviour

We remove unnecessary distance between people.

### Human truth

Something has passed directly from one set of hands to another.

### Evidence

We don't ask people to believe our adjectives.

We show them facts.

### Emotional quality

Quiet confidence.

Not moral superiority.

### Visual consequence

The identity should feel:

* direct
* human
* precise
* tactile
* intelligent
* grounded
* restrained
* distinctive
* quietly confident

It should **not** feel:

* rustic
* NGO-like
* corporate sustainability
* luxury-organic
* wellness
* artisanal cliché
* “eco”
* technological
* folkloric

These are not creative directions.

They are prohibited shortcuts.

### Do not use:

* leaf icons
* hands holding leaves
* hands holding seeds
* globes
* location pins
* mountains
* farms
* trees
* roots
* sprouts

* generic human silhouettes

---

# 06. THE TEST OF ORIGINALITY

Every concept must answer:

### Could another ethical agriculture company use this?

If yes:

**Reject or fundamentally rethink it.**

A logo doesn't become proprietary because we attach an interesting explanation to it.

The underlying form must possess its own character.

A memorable identity often combines simplicity with some form of unusualness or tension. Chermayeff & Geismar specifically describe this balance between uncomplicated form and enough distinction to create a mental “hook.”

---

# 07. TERRITORY 01

# THE HUMAN RECORD

### Starting idea

The tally mark.

But NOT as a literal “five lines = five things” illustration.

The deeper idea is:

> **A human being made a record.**

A mark means:

> I counted this.
>
> I witnessed this.
>
> I was here.
>
> This happened.

This connects naturally with OurOrigin's emphasis on proof and first-hand knowledge.

### Design challenge

Create an OurOrigin gesture inspired by:

* counting
* recording
* marking
* crossing
* completion
* accumulation
* witnessing

The result must eventually become **more OurOrigin than “tally.”**

### Critical question

If the viewer says:

> “That's a tally.”

we haven't finished.

If the viewer says:

> “I don't know what that is, but I remember it.”

we may be getting somewhere.

### Current candidate

The existing five-stroke tally remains Candidate A.

Do not discard it.

But do not protect it either.

---

# 08. TERRITORY 02

# OUR + ORIGIN

This territory investigates the name itself.

OurOrigin is an unusual construction.

It contains:

**OUR**

and

**ORIGIN**

The identity may exist inside the relationship between those words.

Questions:

* Can “Our” and “Origin” visually interact?
* Can two forms become one?
* Can an element cross the boundary?
* Can the typography express shared ownership?
* Can the distinction between “our” and “origin” remain visible but connected?
* Can the capital O become structural rather than decorative?

The goal is not a clever typographic trick.

The goal is to discover whether the **name itself contains the strongest visual identity**.

This territory must include serious wordmark exploration.

---

# 09. TERRITORY 03

# THE FIRST-HAND TRACE

Do not draw a hand.

Investigate what a hand leaves behind.

A first hand creates:

* pressure
* trace
* movement
* interruption
* indentation
* stroke
* transfer
* evidence
* imperfection

The visual language could therefore emerge from **contact rather than depiction**.

This territory should feel physical.

Something was made.

Something was touched.

Something was transferred.

The mark should feel like a human action rather than a computer-generated symbol.

---

# 10. TERRITORY 04

# ORIGIN POINT

Investigate origin as an event rather than a location.

Not:

> map pin.

Not:

> globe.

Not:

> coordinates icon.

Instead:

> **something begins here.**

Possible visual principles:

* point
* emergence
* departure
* first movement
* source
* centre
* beginning
* expansion
* trace

The final form may be extremely abstract.

That is acceptable.

The concept should live in the behaviour of the form, not in an obvious pictogram.

---

# 11. TERRITORY 05

# THE WORDMARK AS THE IDENTITY

This territory is mandatory.

We must investigate whether **OurOrigin itself is strong enough to become the primary identity**.

The existing specification already identifies Cormorant Garamond as a temporary proxy and calls for a proprietary hand-drawn wordmark.

That direction should now become more ambitious.

Do not simply modify a font.

Do not merely replace the dots over the “i”.

Design the word:

> **OurOrigin**

as proprietary lettering.

Investigate:

* O proportions
* lowercase rhythm
* r construction
* g construction
* i proportions
* terminal shapes
* stroke contrast
* spacing
* capital/lowercase relationship
* relationship between the two O's
* relationship between “Our” and “Origin”

The goal is not “custom typography” for its own sake.

The goal is:

> **If another company types OurOrigin in a standard font, it should not look like OurOrigin.**

That is a strong sign of ownership.

Custom type is increasingly used by major identity systems precisely because typography can become a distinctive voice rather than merely a selected font.

---

# 12. SHOULD WE EVEN HAVE A SYMBOL?

This question must remain open.

A symbol is not automatically superior to a wordmark.

A symbol introduces another thing the audience must learn.

The design team should therefore prove why OurOrigin needs one.

If the wordmark becomes sufficiently distinctive, the symbol may become:

* secondary
* abbreviated
* product-level
* favicon-level
* stamp-level
* motion-level

rather than the primary identity.

This is consistent with established identity practice: symbols are most useful when there is a compelling strategic reason for an additional visual shorthand.

---

# 13. LOGO THEORY: WHAT WE ARE OPTIMISING FOR

The final mark should be judged against seven primary criteria.

## 1. Appropriateness

Does it belong to OurOrigin?

Not merely agriculture.

OurOrigin.

## 2. Distinctiveness

Can it be confused with another brand?

## 3. Memorability

Can someone remember its silhouette after seeing it briefly?

## 4. Simplicity

Can unnecessary elements be removed without weakening it?

## 5. Ownability

Could we build a trademarkable visual territory around it?

## 6. Reproducibility

Can it survive:

* screen
* print
* stamp
* emboss
* engraving
* packaging
* fabric
* signage
* photocopy

## 7. Longevity

Would we be embarrassed by it in 2036?

The best identity systems are not simply “minimal.”

They are **purposeful, adaptable and consistent while remaining capable of variation.**

---

# 14. THE 3-SECOND TEST

Show the mark for approximately three seconds.

Remove it.

Ask:

> What do you remember?

Then:

> Can you draw the basic structure?

A good mark does not need to be literally drawable from memory.

But it should leave a **specific visual residue**.

---

# 15. THE SILHOUETTE TEST

Fill the entire mark black.

No internal colour.

No texture.

No explanation.

Does the silhouette have personality?

If the answer is no, the mark is relying too heavily on surface treatment.

---

# 16. THE EXPLANATION TEST

Show the logo without explaining the concept.

If the design only becomes impressive after the designer explains:

> “This represents…”

then we have a problem.

The explanation should **deepen** the mark.

It should not **rescue** it.

---

# 17. THE CATEGORY TEST

Place the logo beside:

* organic food
* ethical agriculture
* fair trade
* premium grocery
* sustainable brands
* artisanal food
* farm-to-table brands

Ask:

> Does OurOrigin disappear into the category?

If yes, reject.

---

# 18. THE BAD-PRINT TEST

Print the logo:

* very small
* black ink
* cheap paper
* low-quality printer

If it survives, the geometry is doing the work.

---

# 19. THE MATERIAL TEST

Test every finalist as:

### Paper

Ink / blind emboss / deboss

### Packaging

Kraft / white / coloured

### Physical

Rubber stamp / engraving

### Digital

16px / 24px / 32px / avatar

### Environmental

Large signage

### Motion

Opening/closing gesture

A mark that only works as an Instagram mockup is not a finished identity.

---

# 20. OPTICAL CONSTRUCTION

The final logo must not be governed purely by mathematical geometry.

Mathematical precision and visual precision are different.

The designer must inspect:

* optical centre
* stroke weight
* negative space
* counter size
* spacing
* balance
* apparent alignment
* visual mass
* small-size behaviour

The existing specification already introduces optical-size variants for the tally mark.

Keep that discipline.

But apply it to the **final identity**, not only the current concept.

---

# 21. HUMAN IMPERFECTION VS BAD CRAFT

This distinction is critical.

We want:

> human character.

We do not want:

> sloppy drawing.

A designer must know the difference.

An irregular stroke is meaningful only when it contributes to:

* gesture
* rhythm
* tension
* personality
* memory.

Random wobble is not “human.”

It is just poor construction.

---

# 22. THE CURRENT TALLY: WHAT TO KEEP

The strongest conceptual aspect of the existing tally is not its five strokes.

It is the idea that:

> **The final gesture completes something.**

The current system assigns the diagonal stroke to “You” and describes it as closing the count.

That is worth preserving as a philosophical possibility.

But we should ask:

> Is there a more original visual expression of participation?

Perhaps.

Perhaps not.

Let the design process answer.

---

# 23. COLOUR

The current brand system establishes:

**Bone:** #EFE6D3
**Deep Forest:** #0C1509
**Mark Gold:** #C99A5B

These are strong starting colours and fit the intended warm humanist direction.

However:

## The logo must first work without colour.

Develop every concept in:

> Black / White.

Only after the geometry is approved should colour become part of the identity decision.

Gold must never be used to compensate for weak form.

---

# 24. TYPOGRAPHIC PHILOSOPHY

The current system proposes three registers:

### Narrative

Humanist / literary typography

### Evidence

Monospaced technical typography

### Display

A distinct display voice

That architecture is strategically useful.

The final identity should preserve this conceptual tension:

> **human story + hard evidence**

That contrast is one of OurOrigin's strongest visual opportunities.

The identity should therefore not become uniformly “organic.”

It needs a precise side.

# 26. THE VISUAL SYSTEM MUST NOT DEPEND ON THE LOGO

The logo is the signature.

The **identity system** is the language.

Once the mark is established, its underlying characteristics may become:

* dividers
* patterns
* animation
* crops
* framing devices
* packaging structures
* data graphics
* photography compositions
* editorial elements
* motion principles

This is how the identity becomes larger than the logo.

Contemporary identity work often treats a core mark or gesture as the basis for a broader system rather than a standalone asset. Pentagram's work for EDP, for example, develops a logo-generated movement into a broader dynamic language.

---

# 27. MOTION SHOULD COME FROM THE MARK

Do not invent unrelated logo animation.

Ask:

> **How was this mark made?**

Then animate that behaviour.

If it is a record:

→ it is recorded.

If it is a connection:

→ two things meet.

If it is a trace:

→ something passes across a surface.

If it is an origin:

→ something emerges.

The animation should reveal the identity's underlying physical logic.

---

# 28. PHOTOGRAPHY SHOULD NOT "EXPLAIN" THE LOGO

Avoid:

logo = tally

photography = farmer's hand

graphic = leaf

background = field

This becomes repetitive symbolism.

Instead, photography should show the **real world**.

The logo can remain abstract.

The real people and places carry the literal truth.

This is consistent with OurOrigin's existing philosophy of showing origin rather than merely selling a story about it.

| ------------------- | -----: |

| Ownability          |    20% |
| Memorability        |    15% |
| Strategic relevance |    15% |
| Wordmark quality    |    10% |

### Important:

A beautiful logo scoring 10/10 aesthetically but 4/10 on distinctiveness should lose.

We are not decorating a brand.

---

# 34. THE FOUNDER'S FINAL QUESTION

Do not ask:

> “Do I like it?”

Ask:

> **“If this became the visual signature of OurOrigin for the next twenty years, would I recognise it as ours?”**

Then:

> **“Could anyone else plausibly own this?”**

Then:

> **“Would it still work if every explanation disappeared?”**

If all three answers are strong:

We may have the identity.

---

# 35. THE STANDARD

OurOrigin should not look like:

> another ethical food brand.

It should look like:

> **a new category of relationship between origin and ownership.**

The visual identity should feel as if it has been **discovered**, not decorated.

It should be simple enough to become familiar.

Strange enough to become memorable.

Human enough to feel made.

Precise enough to feel trustworthy.

Quiet enough to avoid advertising itself.

Distinctive enough to eventually need no explanation.

**Do not design a logo about farming.**

**Design a mark about OurOrigin.**
